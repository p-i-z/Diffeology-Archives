\chapter{A Primer on Diffeology}
\label{chap:A Primer on Diffeology}

The first part of this Memoir is dedicated to the construction of the \emph{Quantum Broth}.
Before the distillation of the prequantum groupoid can occur,
we must establish the geometric reality of the space of dynamical fluctuations.
This stage is \emph{primordial} because it deals with the raw,
unfiltered material of the quantum system:
the infinite-dimensional space of all smooth paths.

We begin by providing the foundational grammar of \emph{Diffeology}.
This language is essential to treat the space of paths not as a formal limit or a measure-theoretic ghost,
but as a rigorous,
smooth geometric object.
Once this language is established,
we endow the broth with its canonical \emph{Action Form} $\Komega$.
This form,
derived from the classical parasymplectic structure,
serves as the universal potential that will eventually guide the filtration into the space of quantum motions.

Classical differential geometry,
for all its power,
lives in constant fear of a self-inflicted nightmare:
the \emph{singularity}.
The rigid requirement that every point in a space must look locally like a Euclidean space forces any object that violates this rule to be treated as a "pathology."
This has led to the creation of a host of complex,
bespoke theories designed to handle these exceptions:
the intricate gluing rules of \emph{orbifolds},
the axiomatic frameworks of \emph{stratifications},
the elaborate classifications of the \emph{singularities of differentiable maps},
and the dramatic bestiary of \emph{catastrophe theory}.
Each is a testament to the immense effort required to study singular phenomena when one's foundational tools are designed to forbid them.

Diffeology proposes a simpler,
more direct path.
It suggests that the nightmare is not a feature of the world,
but an artifact of our tools.
Instead of building a geometry that excludes singularities and then struggling to add them back in,
diffeology begins with a more fundamental and flexible definition of smoothness.

The result is a framework where singularities are not a problem to be solved,
but a natural and integral part of the geometry itself.
\emph{Once a diffeology is defined,
it comes with its singularities already incrusted in its structure},
revealed by the action of its group of local symmetries.

This is not a mere desire for mathematical generalization.
It is a direct response to the demands of physics.
As we see in this Memoir,
singularities appear,
uninvited,
when we ask the most basic physical questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item What is the space of all possible trajectories for a particle in a periodic universe?
  The answer,
  the space of geodesics on a torus,
  is a singular space.
  \item What is the true space of states in a theory with symmetries?
  The answer,
  a quotient space,
  is often singular.
  \item How do we describe the structure of a \emph{quasicrystal}\,?
  The answer requires a geometry that can handle the dense,
  non-periodic symmetries of an irrational torus.
\end{itemize}

Classical differential geometry is not equipped to speak about these objects in a unified,
intrinsic way.
Diffeology is.
This chapter provides the foundational grammar of this new,
more powerful language.
It is built on the Kleinian principle that a geometry is the study of a space and its group of symmetries%
\footnote{A Kleinian principle that does not reduce the geometry to "Topology plus Algebra" as it is the case in ordinary differential geometry,
but defines it as the pair formed by the diffeological space and its diffeological symmetries,
which is deduced from it but cannot be separated from it.}
---a principle that,
as we will see,
applies with equal force to the smooth and the singular alike.

\section{The Language of Plots}
\label{sec:The Language of Plots}

The fundamental idea of diffeology is to define the smoothness of a space not by charts mapping \emph{out} to $\fieldR^n$,
but by test curves (or surfaces, or volumes etc.) mapping \emph{in} from Euclidean domains.

Let $X$ be a set.
A \emph{parametrization} in $X$ is any map $P: U \to X$,
where $U$ is an open domain of some Euclidean space $\fieldR^n$.
A \emph{diffeology} on $X$ is a chosen set $\cD$ of these parametrizations,
which satisfies three axioms that formalize the intuitive notion of "smooth consistency."

\begin{itemize}
  \item[D1.]\textbf{Covering.}
  The set $\cD$ contains all constant parametrizations.
  This ensures that every point of the space is "touched" by the diffeology.
  \item[D2.] \textbf{Locality.}
  Smoothness is a local property.
  If a parametrization $P: U \to X$ is such that every point in $U$ has a neighborhood restricted to which $P$ belongs to $\cD$,
  then $P$ itself belongs to $\cD$.
  \item[D3.] \textbf{Smooth Compatibility.}
  Smoothness is stable under smooth re\-pa\-ra\-me\-tri\-za\-tion.
  If $P: U \to X$ is in $\cD$,
  and $F: V \to U$ is a standard smooth map ($\Cinfty$) between Euclidean domains,
  then the composition $P \circ F$ is in $\cD$.
\end{itemize}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Comparing diffeologies
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Comparing diffeologies]
  We say that a diffeology $\cD$ on a set $X$ is \emph{finer} than a diffeology $\cD'$ if $\cD \subset \cD'$.
  In this case we say that $\cD'$ is \emph{coarser} than $\cD$.
  For this partial order the set of diffeologies on a set is a \emph{complete lattice}.
  
  \textbf{Note.}~Every set has a minimum: the \emph{discrete diffeology} whose plots are locally constant parametrizations,
  and a maximum: the \emph{coarse diffeology} whose plots are all parametrizations
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Diffeological spaces
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Diffeological spaces]
  A set $X$ equipped with a diffeology $\cD$ is called a \emph{diffeological space}.
  The elements of $\cD$ are called the \emph{plots} of the space.
  They are the "probes" we use to explore the geometry.
  A space equipped with the discrete diffeology is said to be \emph{discrete},
  and a set equipped with the coarse diffeology is said to be \emph{trivial} or \emph{coarse}.
\end{article}

The category of diffeological spaces is closed under four fundamental operations:
sums,
products, subset and quotient.
These constructions are essential for building global objects from
local data and for defining functional spaces later.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Sum of diffeological spaces
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Sum of diffeological spaces]
  \label{subsec:sums}
  Let $\{X_i\}_{i \in \cI}$ be any family of diffeological spaces.
  There exists a finest diffeology on the sum $X = \coprod_{X_i \in \cI} X_i$ such that each injection $j_i = x \mapsto (i,x)$,
  from $X_i$ to $X$,
  is smooth.
  We call this the \emph{sum diffeology}.
  The plots of $X$ are the parameterization $r \mapsto (i_r,x_r)$ such that $r \mapsto i_r$ is locally constant.
  
  Technically,
  a parametrization $P : U \to X$ is a plot if and only if for all points $r \in U$ there exist an index $i$ and a neighborhood $V$ of $r$ such that $P \restriction V$ is a plot in $X_i$.
  
  \noindent
  \textit{Intuition.} A smoothly parametrized family in a disjoint union
  must stay within a single component on each connected component of the domain.
  It cannot jump between different $X_i$'s wildly.
  This condition ensures that the sum diffeology is the natural one:
  it satisfies the universal property of the categorical coproduct.
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Product of diffeological spaces
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Product of diffeological spaces]
  \label{subsec:product}
  Let $\{X_i\}_{i \in \cI}$ be any family of diffeological spaces.
  There exists on the product $X = \prod_{i \in \cI} X_i$ a coarsest diffeology such that every projection $\pi_i \colon X \to X_i$ is smooth.
  (Note that the discrete diffeology,
  though finest,
  also makes the projections smooth;
  the product diffeology is the \emph{coarsest} among all diffeologies with this property.)
  It is called the \emph{product diffeology}.
  A parametrization $P: U \to \prod_{i\in I} X_i$ is a plot if and only if
  for every index $i\in I$, the composition
  $\pr_i \circ P : U \to X_i$ is a plot of $X_i$,
  where $\pr_i$ denotes the projection onto the $i$-th factor.
  This condition characterizes the product diffeology uniquely.
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Subsets and discrete subsets
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Subsets and discrete subsets]
  Every subset $A \subset X$ inherits a natural structure called the \emph{subset diffeology}.
  It is the coarsest diffeology on $A$ such that the injection $j : A \to X$ is smooth.
  The plots of $A$ are simply the plots of $X$ that take their values entirely within $A$.
  
  A particularly important case is the \emph{discrete diffeology}.
  As we say above,
  a space is discrete if its only plots are the locally constant maps.
  This is the structure naturally inherited by the group of periods:
  $\Pomega \subset \fieldR$ is discrete if and only if $\Pomega \neq \fieldR$,
  that is,
  even when $\Pomega$ is dense which the general case.
  In the special case $\Pomega = a \ZZ$,
  $\Pomega$ is not just \emph{induced} in $\fieldR$ but \emph{embedded},
  see below.
\end{article}


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Quotient diffeology and quotient spaces
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Quotient diffeology and quotient spaces]
  Let $X$ be a diffeological space and $\sim$ an equivalence relation.
  There exist a finest diffeology on the quotient space $X' = X/\!\!\sim$ such that the projection $\pi : X \to X'$ is smooth.
  We call it the \emph{quotient diffeology}.
  A plot in $X'$ is any parametrization $P : U \to X'$ such that for all $r \in U$,
  there exist an open neighborhood $V$ of $r$ and a plot $Q : V \to X$ such that $P \restriction V = \pi \circ Q$.
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Generating Families
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Generating Families]
  In practice,
  we rarely define a diffeology by listing every possible plot.
  Instead,
  we provide a \emph{generating family} $\cF$.
  The diffeology generated by $\cF$ is the finest (smallest) diffeology containing $\cF$.
  Its plots are those parametrizations that locally factor through elements of $\cF$ via standard smooth maps.
  This concept is the smooth analogue of a basis for a topology.
  
  In particular,
  let $\cF$ a generating family of $X$,
  we call \emph{nebula} of $\cF$ the sum of the domains of the elements of $\cF$
  $$
    \cN = \sum_{F \in \cF} \Domain(F).
    $$%
  Then $X$ is equivalent to the diffeological quotient of the nebula $\cF$ for the equivalent relation:
  $(F,r) \sim (F',r')$ if $F(r) = F'(r')$.
  
  In particular applying this to the nebula of the diffeology,
  we get that every diffeological space can be reconstructed using the two operations sum and quotient applied to the domains of the plots.
  And since any Euclidean domain is a subset of some multiple product of the real line,
  every diffeological space is the result of these four operations applied in some way to the real line.
  
  \begin{proposition}\textup{(Universality of Diffeology)}
    Every diffeological space can be obtained from the real line $\fieldR$ by
    repeatedly applying the operations of sum, product, subset, and quotient.
    The real line is the primordial geometric atom of diffeology.
  \end{proposition}
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — The Diffeological Dimension
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[The Diffeological Dimension]
  \label{subsec:The Diffeological Dimension}
  
  We now turn to a structural invariant that distinguishes diffeological spaces from one another: \emph{dimension}.
  In diffeology,
  dimension is not a topological property but a measure of the \emph{degrees of freedom} inherent in the smooth structure.
  
  We define the dimension of a diffeological space $X$ using the concept of generating families \cite{PIZ06}.
  Let $\cF$ be a generating family of plots for $X$.
  We define the dimension of a plot $P: U \to X$ as the dimension of its domain,
  $\dim(P) := \dim(U)$.
  The dimension of the family $\cF$ is the supremum of the dimensions of its elements:
  \[
    \dim(\cF) = \sup_{P \in \cF} \dim(P).
    \]
  The global dimension of $X$ is the infimum of the dimensions of all possible generating families:
  \[
    \dim(X) := \inf_{\cF \in \GenFam(X)} \dim(\cF).
    \]
  A space is infinite-dimensional if $\dim(\cF) = \infty$ for all generating families $\cF$.
  
  Intuitively,
  dimension measures the "thickness" of the smooth structure.
  It corresponds to the smallest integer $n$ such that every plot of $X$,
  locally around a point,
  can be factored through a plot defined on an open domain of $\fieldR^n$.
  
  \begin{remark} \textup{(Cartography vs. Navigation)}
    \label{subsec:Cartography vs. Navigation}
    The classical manifold approach is like \emph{Cartography},
    requiring a pre-existing grid (a chart) that maps the world to $\fieldR^n$.
    The diffeological approach is like \emph{Navigation}.
    You define the location by a path,
    mapping a curve \emph{in} from time.
    The geometry is defined not by coordinates,
    but by the collection of all valid itineraries.
    
    This difference in approach has a profound consequence,
    as shown by the \emph{Boman Paradox}:
    Consider the set $\fieldR^2$ equipped with the diffeology generated by all smooth curves $\Cinfty(\fieldR, \fieldR^2)$.
    This \emph{Wire Plane} $\cW$ is topologically identical to $\fieldR^2$ and has the same symmetry group $\Diff(\fieldR^2)$.
    Yet its diffeological dimension is $1$, not $2$.
    This demonstrates that the local smooth structure is a primary geometric ingredient,
    independent of topology or algebra.
    For a full discussion of the Boman paradox and its implications for the foundations of geometry,
    see \cite{PIZ25c}.
  \end{remark}
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — The D-Topology
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[The D-Topology]
  A diffeological space carries a natural topology,
  the \emph{D-topology},
  which is the finest topology making all the plots continuous.
  A subset $A \subset X$ is \emph{open} if and only if for every plot $P: U \to X$,
  the preimage $P^{-1}(A)$ is open in $U$.
  The D-topology is a byproduct of the diffeology;
  it cannot be a substitute for it.
  
  The D-Topology is used in particular to distinguish \emph{embedding} from \emph{inductions}.
  Equipped with the subset diffeology,
  the injection $j: A \to X$ is always an induction.
  When the D-Topolgy of $A$ coincides with the restriction on $A$ of the D-Topology of $X$,
  we have what we call an \emph{embedding}.
  
  For instance, the inclusion $\ZZ \hookrightarrow \fieldR$ is an embedding because
  the D-topology on $\ZZ$ (discrete) coincides with the subspace topology from $\fieldR$.
  In contrast, a dense subgroup $G \subset \fieldR$ (such as $\ZZ + \alpha\ZZ$) is
  induced but not embedded: its D-topology is discrete, but the subspace topology
  from $\fieldR$ is not.
  Actually,
  the only strict subgroup embedded in $\fieldR$ are the subgroup $a\ZZ$ for some $a \in \fieldR$.
\end{article}

\section{Functional Diffeology}
\label{sec:Functional Diffeology}

The theory of diffeological spaces defines a category for which the morphisms are the \emph{smooth maps} and isomorphisms are called \emph{diffeomorphisms}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Smooth Maps and the Category $\Difflg$.
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Smooth Maps and the Category $\Difflg$]
  A map $f: X \to Y$ between two diffeological spaces is \emph{smooth} if it takes plots to plots.
  That is,
  for every plot $P$ of $X$,
  the composition $f \circ P$ is a plot of $Y$.
  A \emph{diffeomorphism} is a smooth bijection with a smooth inverse.
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Inductions and Subductions
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Inductions and Subductions]
  A smooth map $f: X \to Y$ is an \emph{induction} if it is an isomorphism from $X$ onto its image $f(X)$ equipped with the subset diffeology.
  In our construction,
  the classical space $X$ is embedded as the \emph{Skeleton} of the prequantum groupoid via an induction.
  Conversely,
  a map $\pi: X \to Q$ is a \emph{subduction} if its plots are exactly the local push-forwards of plots of $X$.
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Functional Diffeology
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Functional Diffeology]
  \label{subsec:Functional Diffeology}
  The category of diffeological spaces is complete and co-complete,
  that is,
  closed under the four set-theoretic operations,
  and,
  moreover,
  is Cartesian closed.
  The set of smooth maps $\Cinfty(X, Y)$ carries the \emph{functional diffeology}.
  A parametrization $P: U \to \Cinfty(X, Y)$ is a plot if and only if the associated evaluation map $(u, x) \mapsto P(u)(x)$ is smooth.
  And this diffeology satisfies the natural identity $\Cinfty(X,\Cinfty(Y,Z)) \simeq \Cinfty(X \times Y,Z)$.
  
  In particular,
  this allows us to do differential geometry directly on the space $\Paths(X)$ without introducing specific more constructions.
\end{article}

\section{Paths and Space of Paths}
\label{sec:Paths and Space of Paths}

The principal ingredient of the Geometric Quantization by Paths is the space of (smooth) paths,
which we denote by $\Paths(X)$,
for any diffeological space $X$.
It is the space of smooth maps from the real line into the diffeological space,
but what matters is that happens between $0$,
the source,
and $1$,
the target.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — The Space of Paths
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[The Space of Paths]
  Homotopy in diffeology is the study of smooth deformations.
  It is built directly upon the functional structure of the space of paths.
  
  Let $X$ be a diffeological space.
  We denote by
  $$
    \Paths(X) = \Cinfty(\fieldR,X)
    $$%
  the space of smooth paths in $X$,
  equipped with the functional diffeology.
  For all $t \in \fieldR$,
  we define the evaluation map $\hat t$ by:
  $$
    \hat t \in \Cinfty(\Paths(X), X),
    \quad
    \hat t(\gamma) = \gamma(t).
    $$%
  We denote by $\0$ and $\1$ the source and target maps.
  We denote by $\EndsMap$ the \emph{ends} map $\0 \times \1$ from $\Paths(X)$ to $X \times X$:
  $$
    \EndsMap \in \Cinfty(\Paths(X), X \times X),
    \quad
    \EndsMap : \gamma \mapsto (\gamma(0),\gamma(1)).
    $$%
  We denote by $\Paths(X,x,\ast)$,
  $\Paths(X,\ast,x)$,
  and $\Paths(X,x,x')$ the subspaces defined by fixing endpoints:
  \begin{align*}
    \Paths(X,x,\ast) &= \{ \gamma \in \Paths(X) \mid \0(\gamma) = x \},\\
    \Paths(X,\ast,x) &= \{ \gamma \in \Paths(X) \mid \1(\gamma) = x \},\\
    \Paths(X,x,x') &= \{ \gamma \in \Paths(X) \mid \EndsMap(\gamma) = (x,x'). \}
  \end{align*}
  We denote by $\Loops(X)$ the space of free loops of $X$,
  $$
    \Loops(X) = \{ \ell \in \Paths(X) \mid \1(\ell)=\0(\ell) \},
    $$%
  and we denote by $\Loops(X,x)$ the set of loops based at $x$,
  $$
    \Loops(X,x) = \Paths(X,x,x) = \{ \ell \in \Loops(X) \mid \0(\ell) = x \}.
    $$%
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Stationary Paths
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Stationary Paths]
  Many constructions in homotopy involve concatenating paths.
  For $\gamma$ and $\gamma'$ such that $\1(\gamma) = \0(\gamma')$,
  the standard concatenation $\gamma \vee \gamma'$ is defined by:
  $$
    \gamma \vee \gamma': t \mapsto
    \begin{cases}
    \gamma(2t) \widetext{if} t \leq 1/2, \\
    \gamma'(2t-1) \widetext{if} t \geq 1/2.
    \end{cases}
    $$%
  Similarly,
  for any path $\gamma \in \Paths(X)$,
  the reversed path is defined by $\bar{\gamma}(t) = \gamma(1-t)$.
  However,
  this concatenation is not necessarily smooth at $t=1/2$.
  This is why we consider the concatenation on the subspace of \emph{stationary paths} \cite[\textsection 5.4]{PIZ13}.
  A path $\gamma \in \Paths(X)$ is (strongly) stationary if there exists an $\epsilon > 0$ such that:
  $$
    \gamma \restriction \left]-\infty, +\epsilon\right[ = \gamma(0)
    \widetext{and}
    \gamma \restriction \left]1 -\epsilon, + \infty \right[ = \gamma(1).
    $$%
  Every path is fixed-ends homotopic to a stationary path by composing with the \emph{smashing function} $\lambda$,
  $\gamma \mapsto \gamma^* = \gamma \circ \lambda$ (see Figure \ref{fig:smashing-function}).
  This is sufficient to ensure that many operations in diffeology involving the concatenation of paths,
  and depending only on their homotopy class,
  are justified.
  Note that if $\gamma$ is a stationary path,
  its reversal $\bar{\gamma}$ is also stationary,
  ensuring that the space of stationary paths is closed under both concatenation and reversal.

  
  %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
  \begin{figure}[tb]\renewcommand{\thefigure}{$1$}
    \smallskip  \centerline{\includegraphics[width=.25\textwidth]{Figures/fig-smashing-function}}
    \vspace{-.5ex}
    \caption{The smashing function $\lambda$.}
    \label{fig:smashing-function}
  \end{figure}
  %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
  
  \textbf{Convention.}
  To avoid unnecessary complications in the following,
  we shall consider only stationary paths without explicitly mentioning them.
  We will not change the notations so as not to make the text more cumbersome.
  That is,
  $\Paths(X)$ will denote the set of stationary paths,
  $\Loops(X)$ the space of stationary loops,
  etc.
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Homotopy and Connectivity
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Homotopy and Connectivity]
  Two points $x, x' \in X$ are \emph{connected} if there exists a smooth path $\gamma \in \Paths(X)$ such that $\gamma(0) = x$ and $\gamma(1) = x'$.
  The set of connected components $\pi_0(X)$ is the quotient of $X$ by this smooth connectivity.
  We denote by $\Comp$ the projection:
  $$
    \Comp: \Loops(X) \to \pi_0(\Loops(X)).
    $$%
  Similarly,
  two smooth maps $f, g: X \to Y$ are \emph{homotopic} if there exists a smooth path in the functional space $\Cinfty(X, Y)$ connecting them.
  This is equivalent to the existence of a smooth map $H: \fieldR \times X \to Y$ such that $H(0, \cdot) = f$ and $H(1, \cdot) = g$.
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Recursive Homotopy Groups
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Recursive Homotopy Groups]
  The recursive nature of homotopy groups is particularly transparent in diffeology.
  Since the space of based loops $\Loops(X, x)$ is itself a diffeological space,
  we can define higher homotopy groups by iteration.
  For any $k \geq 1$,
  we have the recursive relation:
  $$
    \pi_k(X, x) = \pi_{k-1}(\Loops(X, x), \hat{x}),
    $$%
  where $\hat{x}$ is the constant loop at $x$.
  By induction,
  this identifies the $k$-th homotopy group with the set of connected components of the $k$-th iterated loop space:
  $$
    \pi_k(X, x) = \pi_0(\Loops_k(X,x), \hat{x}_k).
    $$%
  This approach treats higher homotopy not as a separate topological construction,
  but as the connectivity of increasingly complex functional spaces.
  In this Memoir,
  the group $\pi_2(X, x)$ is essential for capturing the \emph{spherical periods} of the action form,
  which arise from the integration of $\omega$ over smooth $2$-spheres.
  For most spaces of interest,
  these groups are \emph{discrete},
  meaning their plots are locally constant.
  This reflects the fact that smooth deformations cannot jump between distinct homotopy classes.
  
  The power of this definition is revealed in singular spaces.
  For the \emph{irrational torus} $T_\alpha$,
  the D-topology is coarse and the topological fundamental group is trivial.
  However,
  the diffeological fundamental group is $\pi_1(T_\alpha) \simeq \ZZ^2$,
  capturing the dense windings that topology misses.
  In this Memoir,
  this homotopic precision allows us to define the quantum phase even in systems where the classical topology is "blind."
\end{article}

\section{Differential forms and the de Rham complex in diffeology.}
\label{sec:Differential forms and the de Rham complex in diffeology}

Diffeology support natively a rich exterior differential calculus which we call \emph{Cartan-de-Rham Calculus}.
Here are its elementary constructions.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Differential Forms
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Differential Forms]
  To do physics,
  we need differential calculus.
  In diffeology,
  a differential $k$-form $\alpha$ is defined in the most natural way possible:
  it is a rule that assigns to every plot $P: U \to X$ a standard smooth $k$-form $\alpha(P)$ on the Euclidean domain $U$.
  
  Formally,
  let $k \geq 0$.
  A differential $k$-form $\alpha \in \DForms^k(X)$ is a map that associates to each plot $P: U \to X$ (where $U \subset \fieldR^n$ is a Cartesian domain) a smooth form $\alpha(P) \in \Cinfty(U, \Lambda^k(\fieldR^n))$.
  This assignment must satisfy the \emph{compatibility condition} (the chain rule):
  for any smooth map $F: V \to U$ between Euclidean domains,
  \[
    \alpha(P \circ F) = F^*(\alpha(P)).
    \]
  The space $\DForms^0(X)$ of $0$-forms coincides with the space of smooth real-valued functions $\Cinfty(X, \fieldR)$.
  
\end{article}
\begin{article}\artlabel[Standard Operations]
  Because forms are defined by their local behavior on plots,
  the standard operations of calculus extend immediately to diffeology.
  They are defined \emph{plot-wise}.
  
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Exterior Derivative.}
    The operator $\der: \DForms^k(X) \to \DForms^{k+1}(X)$ is defined by:
    \[
      (\der\alpha)(P) = \der(\alpha(P)),
      \]
    where the $\der$ on the right is the standard exterior derivative on $U$.
    Since $\der \circ \der = 0$ locally,
    it holds globally on $X$.
    This leads to the De Rham cohomology groups $\HDR^k(X) = \ZDR^k(X) / \BDR^k(X)$
    quotient of closed by exact differential forms.
    
    \item \textbf{Wedge Product.}
    The product $\wedge: \DForms^k(X) \times \DForms^l(X) \to \DForms^{k+l}(X)$ is defined by:
    \[
      (\alpha \wedge \beta)(P) = \alpha(P) \wedge \beta(P).
      \]
    That makes the de Rham complex a graded algebra.
    
    \item \textbf{Pullback.}
    Let $f: X \to Y$ be a smooth map and $\beta \in \DForms^k(Y)$.
    The pullback $f^*\beta \in \DForms^k(X)$ is defined by:
    \[
      (f^*\beta)(P) = \beta(f \circ P),
      \]
    for any plot $P$ of $X$.
    This definition is well-posed because,
    by the definition of a smooth map,
    $f \circ P$ is a plot of $Y$.
    Also note that,
    for all differential form $\alpha \in \DForms^k(X)$,
    $\alpha(P) \simeq P^*(\alpha)$.
  \end{enumerate}
  
  \textbf{Note on Terminology.}
  In this Memoir,
  we relax the standard symplectic terminology.
  A \emph{parasymplectic form} on a diffeological space $X$ is simply a closed $2$-form $\omega \in \DForms^2(X)$ (i.e., $\der\omega = 0$).
  The pair $(X, \omega)$ is called a \emph{parasymplectic space}.
  No condition of non-degeneracy is assumed,
  as such a condition is often too restrictive for the infinite-dimensional or singular spaces we consider (like the Quantum Broth).
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — The Descent Criterion
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[The Descent Criterion]
  In diffeology,
  the quotient $Q = X/\!\!\sim$ is always well-defined via the subduction $\pi: X \to Q$.
  This allows us to study spaces like the \emph{irrational torus} $T_\alpha \simeq \fieldR / (\ZZ + \alpha\ZZ)$,
  which is topologically coarse but diffeologically rich.
  
  A form $\beta$ on $X$ descends to a unique form $\alpha$ on the quotient $Q$
  (such that $\pi^*\alpha = \beta$)
  if and only if for any two plots $P, P'$ in $X$ such that $\pi \circ P = \pi \circ P'$,
  we have:
  \[
    \beta(P) = \beta(P').
    \]
  This criterion is the "magic wand" of symplectic diffeology.
  It allows us to push symplectic forms onto singular spaces,
  provided the "local data" of the form agrees on the fibers.
\end{article}

\section{The Chain-Homotopy Operator.}
\label{sec:The Chain-Homotopy Operator}

We conclude this primer by exploring the functional geometry of the space of paths.
This section introduces the essential bridge between the static geometry of the base space and the dynamic geometry of paths.
The central tool here is the \emph{chain-homotopy operator},
which allows us to transgress differential forms from the space of motions to the space of paths.
This construction is not merely a technical convenience;
it is the geometric engine that generates the action potential of the system.
By treating the space of paths as a first-class diffeological object,
we prepare the ground for the construction of the \emph{Quantum Broth} and the subsequent distillation of the \emph{Prequantum Groupoid}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — The Chain-Homotopy Operator
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[The Chain-Homotopy Operator]
  \label{subsec:The Chain-Homotopy Operator}
  The bridge between the classical geometry of $X$ and the functional geometry of $\Paths(X)$ is the \emph{chain-homotopy operator} $\CHO$ \cite{PIZ13}.
  This operator is canonical;
  it requires no choices of coordinates or gauges.
  It maps $k$-forms on $X$ to $(k-1)$-forms on $\Paths(X)$:
  \[
    \CHO : \DForms^k(X) \to \DForms^{k-1}(\Paths(X)).
    \]
  To define $\CHO$ rigorously,
  we use the plot-based definition of differential forms.
  Let $P: U \to \Paths(X)$ be a plot of the space of paths.
  The associated evaluation map $\Phi: \fieldR \times U \to X$,
  defined by $\Phi(t,r) = P(r)(t)$,
  is smooth.
  It is often intuitive to denote the path $P(r)$ by $\gamma_r$,
  so that $\Phi(t,r) = \gamma_r(t)$.
  
  The form $\CHO\alpha$ is defined by its value on the plot $P$ at a point $r \in U$.
  For any vectors $v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}$ in the tangent space of $U$ at $r$:
  \[
    (\CHO\alpha)(P)_r(v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}) = \int_0^1 \alpha\left(\Vector{t \\ r} \mapsto P(r)(t) \right)_{t \choose r}\Vector{1 \\ 0} \Vector{0 \\ v_1} \dots \Vector{0 \\ v_{k-1}} \dt.
    \]
  Note that,
  for multilinear operators,
  we use the notations $\epsilon(v_1)\dots(v_k)$ and $\epsilon(v_1,\dots,v_k)$ indifferently.
  
  Geometrically,
  this corresponds to integrating $\alpha$ along the variation of the path.
  The operator $\CHO$ satisfies the fundamental \emph{chain-homotopy identity}:
  \[
    \der \circ \CHO + \CHO \circ \der = \1^* - \0^*,
    \]
  where $\0, \1: \Paths(X) \to X$ are the source and target maps defined by $\0(\gamma) = \gamma(0)$ and $\1(\gamma) = \gamma(1)$.
  This identity is the "fundamental theorem of calculus" for the space of paths,
  relating the exterior derivative of the transgressed form to the values of the original form at the endpoints.
  
  \textbf{Remark.}~If $X$ is a smooth manifold,
  the tangent space to $\Paths(X)$ at a path $\gamma$ can be identified with the space of vector fields along $\gamma$.
  Let $\delta\gamma_1, \dots, \delta\gamma_{k-1}$ be $k-1$ such variations.
  The formula for $\CHO\alpha$ takes the familiar form:%
  \footnote{In the literature, this is sometimes abbreviated as $\Kalpha = \int_0^1 i_{\dot{\gamma}} \alpha \, dt$, where $i_{\dot{\gamma}}$ denotes the contraction with the velocity vector field along the curve.}
  \[
    (\CHO\alpha)_\gamma(\delta\gamma_1, \dots, \delta\gamma_{k-1}) = \int_0^1 \alpha_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t), \delta\gamma_1(t), \dots, \delta\gamma_{k-1}(t)) \, dt.
    \]
  It is important to note that this intuitive formula is not an ad-hoc adaptation;
  it is the strict evaluation of the general diffeological definition on the specific plots of a manifold.
  However,
  we prefer the plot-based definition because it applies universally
  ---to singular spaces,
  infinite-dimensional spaces,
  and quotients---
  where the classical notion of a tangent vector field may be ill-defined.
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article —
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Homotopic Invariance of de Rham Cohomology]
  The chain-homotopy identity provides a spectacular and immediate proof of the homotopic invariance of de Rham cohomology.
  Let $f_0, f_1: X \to Y$ be two homotopic smooth maps,
  and let $\hat{h}: X \to \Paths(Y)$ be the map $x \mapsto [t \mapsto f_t(x)]$,
  associating to each point $x \in X$ the path of the homotopy starting at $f_0(x)$ and ending at $f_1(x)$.
  For any closed form $\omega \in \DForms^k(Y)$,
  the identity $\1^*\omega - \0^*\omega = \der\CHO\omega$ leads to the following three-line proof:
  \begin{align*}
    f_1^*\omega - f_0^*\omega &= \hat{h}^*(\1^*\omega - \0^*\omega) \\
    &= \hat{h}^*(\der\CHO\omega) \\
    &= \der[\hat{h}^*(\CHO\omega)].
  \end{align*}
  This calculation shows that the difference $f_1^*\omega - f_0^*\omega$ is an exact form.
  Consequently,
  homotopic maps induce the same homomorphism in de Rham cohomology,
  proving that $\HDR$ is a homotopic invariant for all diffeological spaces.
\end{article}

\section{Diffeological Groups}
\label{sec:Diffeological Groups}

A \emph{diffeological group} is a group $G$ where multiplication and inversion are smooth.
Thanks to functional diffeology,
the group of diffeomorphisms $\Diff(X)$ is always a diffeological group.
A plot $P:r \mapsto \phi_r$ in $\Diff(X)$ is a parametrization such that the evaluation maps $(r,x) \mapsto \phi_r(x)$ and $(r,x) \mapsto \phi_r^{-1}(x)$ are smooth.
This allows us to treat symmetry groups of infinite-dimensional spaces with geometric rigor.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — The Space of Momenta of a Diffeological Group
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[The Space of Momenta of a Diffeological Group]
  \label{subsec:Momenta}
  A fundamental construction in this Memoir is the \emph{space of momenta} of a diffeological group.
  We call \emph{left momentum} of a diffeological group $G$ any $1$-form on $G$ invariant by the left action of $G$ onto itself.
  We denote by $\cG^*$ the space of momenta of $G$:
  \[
    \cG^* = \{ \alpha \in \DForms^1(G) \mid \text{for all } g \in G, \; \Lm(g)^*(\alpha) = \alpha \}.
    \]
  The space $\cG^*$ is naturally a diffeological vector space equipped with the functional diffeology.
  
  Note that,
  in spite of what the notation $\cG^*$ suggests,
  the space of momenta of a diffeological group is \emph{not} defined by some duality with a Lie algebra.
  It is defined intrinsically as the space of left-invariant $1$-forms.
  This avoids any reference to contravariant objects like vector fields or Lie algebras,
  which are ill-defined or non-unique in general diffeology.
  
  The group $G$ acts smoothly on $\cG^*$ by the \emph{coadjoint action}:
  \[
    \Ad_* : (g,\alpha) \mapsto \Ad(g)_*(\alpha) = \Ad(g^{-1})^*(\alpha) = \Rm(g)^*(\alpha).
    \]
  The orbit of a momentum under this action is a \emph{coadjoint orbit}.
  When $G$ is a Lie group,
  $\cG^*$ coincides with the dual of the Lie algebra,
  and the coadjoint action coincides with the classical one.
  
  A crucial property used throughout this Memoir is the behavior of \emph{closed} momenta.
  Let $\cZ \subset \cG^*$ denote the subspace of closed left-invariant $1$-forms.
  If $G$ is connected,
  every closed momentum is exact and its primitive is a smooth homomorphism from the universal covering $\UniversalCover{G}$ to $\fieldR$.
  Moreover,
  every closed momentum is invariant under the coadjoint action:
  \[
    d\zeta = 0 \quad\implies\quad \Ad_*(g)(\zeta) = \zeta \quad \text{for all } g \in G.
    \]
  This fact is essential for the construction of the holonomy group in Parts II and IV.
\end{article}

%% MARK:  Article — One-Dimensional Tori and Subgroups of $\fieldR$
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[One-Dimensional Tori and Subgroups of $\fieldR$]
  \label{art:One-Dimensional Tori}
  The construction of the prequantum groupoid in this Memoir hinges on the
  interplay between subgroups of the real line and the diffeological tori
  they define.
  
  A subgroup $\Gamma \subset \fieldR$ is \emph{strict} if $\Gamma \neq \fieldR$.
  Every strict subgroup of $\fieldR$ is \emph{discrete} in the diffeological sense:
  equipped with the subset diffeology,
  its only plots are the locally constant maps.
  This is a theorem of diffeology~\cite{PIZ13},
  and it holds even when $\Gamma$ is dense in $\fieldR$,
  as is the case for $\Gamma = \ZZ + \alpha\ZZ$ with $\alpha \notin \QQ$.
  
  \begin{definition}
    A \emph{one-dimensional torus} is any quotient of the form
    \[
      \Torus = \fieldR/\Gamma,
      \]
    where $\Gamma \subset \fieldR$ is a strict subgroup.
    The projection $\pi: \fieldR \to \Torus$ is a subduction,
    and $\Torus$ is a diffeological group.
  \end{definition}
  
  The standard cases are:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $\Gamma = a\ZZ$ for some $a > 0$.
    Then $\Torus \simeq \Sph^1$ is the standard circle of perimeter $a$.
    The subgroup $\Gamma$ is not just induced but \emph{embedded} in $\fieldR$
    (its D-topology coincides with the subspace topology from $\fieldR$).
    \item $\Gamma = \ZZ + \alpha\ZZ$ with $\alpha \notin \QQ$.
    Then $\Torus = \Torus_\alpha$ is the \emph{irrational torus}.
    It is topologically coarse (its D-topology is the coarse topology)
    but diffeologically rich.
    In particular,
    $\pi_1(\Torus_\alpha) \simeq \ZZ^2$ as a diffeological group.
    \item $\Gamma = \{0\}$.
    Then $\Torus = \fieldR$,
    which can be regarded as the ``circle of infinite radius.''
  \end{enumerate}
  
  On any one-dimensional torus $\Torus = \fieldR/\Gamma$,
  there exists a canonical invariant $1$-form $\theta \in \Omega^1(\Torus)$,
  characterized uniquely by the equation
  \[
    \pi^*\theta = \dt,
    \]
  where $\dt$ is the standard $1$-form on $\fieldR$.
  This form $\theta$ is closed,
  generates the de Rham cohomology of $\Torus$,
  and plays the role of the universal connection form
  on the prequantum torus in Parts~I and~II.
\end{article}

%% MARK:  Article —  Principal Actions and Diffeological Fiber Bundles
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Principal Actions and Diffeological Fiber Bundles]
  \label{art:Principal Diffeological Fiber Bundles}
  
  In classical differential geometry,
  a principal $G$-bundle is a manifold $P$ equipped with a free,
  proper $G$-action whose quotient $P/G$ is locally trivial.
  Diffeology replaces these rigid manifold conditions
  with a single, remarkably powerful plot-based criterion.
  
  Let $G$ be a diffeological group acting smoothly on a diffeological space $P$,
  on the right.
  Consider the \emph{action graph} map
  \[
    \rho: P \times G \to P \times P,
    \qquad
    \rho(p,g) = (p, p \cdot g).
    \]
  
  \begin{definition}
    The action of $G$ on $P$ is \emph{principal} if the action graph $\rho$
    is an \emph{induction}.
    In this case,
    the projection $\pr: P \to X = P/G$ onto the quotient space
    is called a \emph{principal diffeological fibration}
    (or principal diffeological bundle) with structure group $G$.
  \end{definition}
  
  The condition that $\rho$ is an induction means that $\rho$ embeds
  $P \times G$ as a diffeological subspace of $P \times P$.
  This completely replaces the classical requirement of local triviality,
  while simultaneously encoding three essential geometric requirements:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Freeness:} The map is injective on each fiber.
    \item \textbf{Closed orbits:} The image of $\rho$ is diffeologically closed
    (it is a subspace of $P \times P$).
    \item \textbf{Smooth division:} The inverse map $(p, p \cdot g) \mapsto g$
    onto its image is smooth.
  \end{enumerate}
  
  This criterion is immensely versatile.
  It applies without change to finite-dimensional Lie groups,
  to infinite-dimensional groups of diffeomorphisms,
  and to discrete or dense subgroups of $\fieldR$.
  For example,
  the action of the dense subgroup $\ZZ + \alpha\ZZ$ on $\fieldR$ by translation
  satisfies this condition.
  Therefore,
  the projection $\fieldR \to \Torus_\alpha = \fieldR/(\ZZ + \alpha\ZZ)$
  is a principal fibration with fiber $\ZZ + \alpha\ZZ$ ---
  a statement that has no counterpart in classical bundle theory,
  and which gives the irrational torus its rich diffeological structure.
  
  \medskip
  \noindent
  \textbf{Associated Prequantum Bundles.}
  In Parts~I and~II of this Memoir,
  the prequantum groupoid $\Groupoid{T}_\omega$ gives rise to a family of principal bundles
  whose structure group is the torus of periods $\Tomega = \fieldR/\Pomega$.
  For each basepoint $x \in X$,
  the source fiber
  \[
    \cY_x = \Mor_{\Groupoid{T}_\omega}(x,\ast) = \{ [\gamma]_\omega \mid \gamma(0) = x \}
    \]
  is a principal $\Tomega$-bundle over $X$,
  with projection $\1: [\gamma]_\omega \mapsto \gamma(1)$.
  The prequantum $1$-form $\lambda = \blambda|_{\cY_x}$ serves as a connection form
  on this bundle.
  All these bundles are mutually equivalent,
  and any one of them is called a \emph{prequantum bundle}
  associated with the parasymplectic space $(X,\omega)$.
\end{article}

%% MARK:  Article — Connections on Torus Principal Bundles
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Connections on Torus Principal Bundles]
  \label{art:Connections on Torus Principal Bundles}
  
  Let $\Torus = \fieldR/\Gamma$ be a one-dimensional diffeological torus,
  and let $\pr: P \to X$ be a principal $\Torus$-bundle.
  For each $p \in P$,
  the orbit map $\OrbitMap{p}: \Torus \to P$ is defined by
  $\OrbitMap{p}(\tau) = p \cdot \tau$.
  
  \begin{definition}
    A \emph{connection $1$-form} on $P$ is a differential $1$-form
    $\lambda \in \Omega^1(P)$ satisfying two conditions:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Invariance:}
      For all $\tau \in \Torus$,
      $\tau_P^*(\lambda) = \lambda$,
      where $\tau_P: P \to P$ denotes the action of $\tau$.
      \item \textbf{Calibration:}
      For all $p \in P$,
      $\OrbitMap{p}^*(\lambda) = \theta$,
      where $\theta$ is the canonical $1$-form on $\Torus$,
      defined by $\pi_\Torus^*\theta = \dt$.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{definition}
  
  The calibration condition is a normalisation:
  it declares that the connection form measures the fiber direction
  in exactly the same way that $\dt$ measures the real line.
  The invariance condition ensures that $\lambda$ is fully determined
  by its behavior on any local section.
  
  \medskip
  \noindent
  \textbf{Curvature.}
  The exterior derivative $\omega = \der\lambda$ is invariant
  and vanishes on the fiber direction.
  It therefore descends to a unique $2$-form on the base $X$,
  also denoted by $\omega$,
  called the \emph{curvature} of the connection.
  The equation $\pr^*\omega = \der\lambda$ is the structure equation
  of the bundle.
  
  In the GQbP framework,
  the prequantum $1$-form $\lambda = \blambda|_{\cY_x}$ is a connection form
  on the prequantum bundle $\cY_x \to X$,
  and its curvature is precisely the classical parasymplectic form $\omega$.
  This is the geometric content of the prequantization condition:
  the classical $2$-form $\omega$ is realized as the curvature of a connection
  on a principal torus bundle over the space of motions.
\end{article}

\section{Groupoids in Diffeology}
\label{sec:Groupoids in Diffeology}

A \emph{diffeological groupoid} is a groupoid internal to the category of diffeological spaces.
That is, a groupoid $\KK$ for which the set of objects $\Obj(\KK)$ and the set of morphisms $\Mor(\KK)$ are both diffeological spaces,
and all structural maps are smooth:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The source and target maps $\Src, \Trg: \Mor(\KK) \to \Obj(\KK)$ are smooth.
  \item The composition $(f,g) \mapsto f \cdot g$,
  defined on the subset of composable pairs
  $\Mor(\KK) \times_{\Obj(\KK)} \Mor(\KK) = \{ (f,g) \mid \Trg(f) = \Src(g) \}$,
  is smooth.
  \item The inversion $f \mapsto f^{-1}$ is smooth.
  \item The injection $x \mapsto \id_x$ from $\Obj(\KK)$ to $\Mor(\KK)$ is an \emph{induction}.
\end{enumerate}
The last condition ensures that the space of objects is diffeologically identified with the subspace of identity morphisms.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — Fibrating Groupoids
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[Fibrating Groupoids]
  A diffeological groupoid $\KK$ is \emph{fibrating} if the \emph{ends map}
  \[
    \EndsMap: \Mor(\KK) \to \Obj(\KK) \times \Obj(\KK), \quad
    \EndsMap(f) = (\Src(f), \Trg(f)),
    \]
  is a \emph{subduction}.
  A subduction is a smooth surjection such that every plot of the target lifts locally to a plot of the source.
  
  This condition is the diffeological analogue of a groupoid being ``transitive enough''
  to encode a fibration structure.
  When $\KK$ is fibrating,
  the fibers $\Mor_{\KK}(x,x') = \EndsMap^{-1}(x,x')$ are diffeologically homogeneous spaces for the isotropy groups,
  and the projection $\EndsMap$ behaves like a smooth bundle of torsors.
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — The Structure Groupoid of a Projection
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[The Structure Groupoid of a Projection]
  For any smooth surjection $\pi: T \to B$,
  one can define its \emph{structure groupoid} $\KK_\pi$.
  Its objects are the points of the base $B$,
  and its morphisms from $b$ to $b'$ are the diffeomorphisms between the fibers $T_b$ and $T_{b'}$:
  \[
    \Mor_{\KK_\pi}(b,b') = \Diff(T_b, T_{b'}),
    \]
  equipped with the functional diffeology.
  The composition is the usual composition of maps.
  The projection $\pi$ is a \emph{diffeological fibration} if and only if its structure groupoid $\KK_\pi$ is fibrating.
  
  This definition generalizes the classical notion of a fiber bundle to spaces that are not locally trivial in the traditional sense.
  In particular,
  the projection $\pi_\alpha: \Torus^2 \to \Torus_\alpha$ onto the irrational torus
  is a diffeological fibration,
  even though it is not locally trivial topologically.
\end{article}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%% MARK:  Article — The Poincaré Groupoid
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{article}\artlabel[The Poincaré Groupoid]
  Let $X$ be a diffeological space.
  The \emph{Poincaré groupoid} (or fundamental groupoid) $\Pi(X)$ has the points of $X$ as objects
  and the fixed-ends homotopy classes of paths as morphisms:
  \[
    \Mor_{\Pi(X)}(x,x') = \{ [\gamma] \mid \gamma \in \Paths(X), \; \gamma(0)=x,\; \gamma(1)=x' \}/\text{homotopy}.
    \]
  The concatenation of paths defines the composition,
  and the constant path at $x$ serves as the identity $\id_x$.
  
  The Poincaré groupoid is fibrating.
  Its ends map $\EndsMap: \Mor(\Pi(X)) \to X \times X$ is a subduction because every pair of points $(x,x')$
  can be connected by a path,
  and the space of such paths is smoothly parameterized.
  
  For a connected space $X$ with a chosen basepoint $x_0$,
  the universal covering space can be constructed as the space of morphisms starting at $x_0$:
  \[
    \UniversalCover{X} = \Mor_{\Pi(X)}(x_0, \star),
    \]
  with projection $\pi([\gamma]) = \gamma(1)$.
  This construction works for \emph{every} connected diffeological space,
  without any local niceness conditions.
\end{article}
